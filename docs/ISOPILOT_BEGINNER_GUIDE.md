# ISOpilot beginner guide

You do not need to know compliance terminology before using ISOpilot. The platform is organised around a simple idea: understand your organisation, choose what applies, write down how you work, do the work, keep proof, check it, and improve it.

## The seven-step route

### 1. Describe the organisation

Start with the Context page. In plain English, record what the organisation provides, the systems and technology it relies on, the team, the important processes and the customers or service users.

This information matters because a compliance system should describe the real organisation rather than a generic template.

### 2. Choose what applies

Open Documents → ISOpilot Template Library and choose the relevant Fast Start packs.

The Core pack is shared across the management-system standards. It handles common work once, such as context, scope, responsibilities, document control, risk, competence, supplier management, incidents, corrective action, internal audit and management review.

Only select a framework because it is relevant to the organisation, a customer/contract, a regulator, a certification target or a genuine business objective.

### 3. Review the paperwork

ISOpilot creates editable working documents. Do not approve them without reading them.

A **policy** states management direction and commitments. A **procedure** explains how a recurring activity is carried out. A **register** is a maintained list or record, such as risks, suppliers or processing activities. A **plan** defines future activities, owners or targets. A **form/checklist** gives a repeatable structure for a task. A **record** proves that an activity or decision happened.

Not every standard requires a document with every exact title in the library. The documents are practical structures for meeting and evidencing applicable requirements. Keep what is relevant, tailor it to actual practice and justify exclusions or non-applicability where needed.

### 4. Review risks and controls

A requirement says what outcome must be achieved. A control or measure is what the organisation actually does to achieve that outcome or reduce a risk.

Record real risks, not generic ones. Give each material risk an owner, assess it consistently, decide how it will be treated and retain evidence of the treatment and any accepted residual risk.

### 5. Add evidence

A policy is not proof that the organisation follows the policy. Evidence is the real-world record.

Examples include an approved document, training completion record, access review, supplier assessment, signed contract clause, system configuration screenshot, backup/restore test, incident record, completed checklist, customer feedback analysis, waste record, audit report or management-review minutes.

Evidence should be current, relevant to the requirement, attributable to the organisation and protected according to its sensitivity.

### 6. Audit and close gaps

Internal audit checks both conformity and effectiveness. Record findings using objective evidence. Correct the immediate problem, investigate the cause where appropriate, assign corrective action and verify that the action worked before closing it.

### 7. Management review and maintenance

Senior management reviews performance, risks, objectives, audits, incidents, complaints, suppliers, legal/contractual changes, resources and improvement opportunities. Record decisions, owners and due dates.

Compliance is maintained over time. Review the system when the organisation changes and at planned intervals rather than treating certification as a one-off document exercise.

## ISO 9001 version note

As of the 2026-08-07 ISOpilot release baseline, the currently published requirements remain ISO 9001:2015 with Amendment 1:2024. The next edition is under publication and should not be presented to customers as the published ISO 9001:2026 standard until ISO officially publishes it.

## What “audit-ready” means in ISOpilot

Audit-ready does not mean guaranteed certification. It means the organisation can show a coherent system: the applicable requirements are identified, documents describe real practice, risks and controls have owners, evidence is current, internal audits have been performed, findings are controlled, management has reviewed the system and unresolved gaps are visible rather than hidden.

Certification decisions are made by an independent certification body where certification applies. Legal/regulatory compliance also depends on the organisation's actual activities, jurisdictions and obligations.
